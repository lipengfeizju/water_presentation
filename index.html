How is the heat moved out?

01Air-conditioned roomThe simplest option: cool the whole server room with HVAC. Compressors do the work, so it uses a lot of electricity.
02Contain the hot airHot aisles send server exhaust straight back to the air handler.
03Move heat into waterThe air handler transfers the heat into a water loop.
04Evaporate it outdoorsA fluid cooler evaporates water to reject the heat, with little compressor power.

Microsoft Azure, “Modern Datacenter Cooling” (2023), indirect evaporative cooling (IDEC) diagram

Nearly all the electricity becomes heat, so the question is how to carry that heat out of the building. The simplest answer is to put the servers in an air-conditioned room, like an office. That works, but the compressors in the HVAC system use a lot of electricity, so this approach is not efficient.

Modern facilities separate the job into steps. Cool air flows through the server racks. The hot exhaust is contained in a sealed hot aisle and returned to the air handling unit. The air handler moves the heat into a water loop, and the warm water is pumped to a fluid cooler outside. There, water evaporates and carries the heat away. Evaporation does the work that compressors would otherwise do, which saves electricity, and that evaporated water is the onsite water footprint.

Source: Microsoft Azure, Modern Datacenter Cooling infographic, 2023.

Why can modern facilities avoid compressors most of the time? First, servers tolerate warmer air than offices: ASHRAE recommends 18 to 27 °C inlet air, and the allowable classes A1 to A4 extend up to 32 to 45 °C. Second, the outdoor weather decides the cooling mode. When outdoor air is cool, it can be used directly. When it is warm but dry, evaporating water cools it down. Only when it is hot and humid do we need indirect evaporative or mechanical cooling.

First, the metric: power usage effectiveness, PUE, is total facility energy divided by IT equipment energy. A PUE of 1.0 means all electricity goes to computing; 2.0 means one extra kilowatt-hour for cooling and other overhead for every kilowatt-hour of computing.

Here are the main cooling options. Room air-conditioning, or mechanical cooling, has very high power use. Small data centers with direct-expansion units or air-cooled chillers sit around a PUE of 2. That means for every kilowatt-hour of computing, roughly another kilowatt-hour goes to cooling and other overhead.

Using water changed that. Large facilities with economizers and adiabatic or evaporative cooling reach a PUE of about 1.1 to 1.2. This is an important efficiency gain: much less electricity, and therefore less carbon and less pressure on the grid.

The price is water. The evaporative options use from a fraction of a liter up to about two liters per kilowatt-hour, depending on the climate. So the efficiency gain on the power side shows up as water demand, which is the topic of the rest of this talk.

Next, let's discuss the water footprint of AI models, which comprises two main components.

Li, Pengfei, Jianyi Yang, Mohammad A. Islam, and Shaolei Ren. “Making AI Less ‘Thirsty’: Uncovering and Addressing the Secret Water Footprint of AI Models.” Communications of the ACM, 2025.

First, there's offsite water consumption. Similar to Scope 2 carbon emissions, this refers to the water evaporated during the generation of electricity used by the AI models. Consequently, the type of fuel source significantly impacts this water usage.

Second is onsite water consumption for cooling data centers. Modern data centers typically use a two-loop system. The inner loop circulates water to draw heat away from servers and transfer it to an external heat exchanger, consuming no water in this process. The outer loop then dissipates this heat into the atmosphere, often through water evaporation – much like how sweating cools the our body. While air-cooled chillers can reduce this onsite water usage in cooler climates, they often cannot meet the cooling demands in most locations due to climate limitations.

Li, Pengfei, Jianyi Yang, Mohammad A. Islam, and Shaolei Ren. “Making AI Less ‘Thirsty’: Uncovering and Addressing the Secret Water Footprint of AI Models.” Communications of the ACM, 2025.

Therefore, the total water footprint of AI computation is the sum of its onsite and offsite water consumption. Since both onsite and offsite water usage are proportional to energy consumption, we can factor out energy to define a key metric: Water Usage Efficiency, or WUE. We'll denote onsite WUE as WUE_on and offsite WUE as WUE_off.

Pengfei Li, Jianyi Yang, Mohammad A. Islam, Shaolei Ren, "Making AI Less 'Thirsty': Uncovering and Addressing the Secret Water Footprint of AI Models," 2023.

Annual totals and local water capacity

01How much water do data centers need over a year?
02What must the local water system be able to deliver on the hottest day?

Small Bottle, Big Pipe: Quantifying and Addressing the Impact of Data Centers on Public Water Systems

Yuelin Han, Pengfei Li, Adam Wierman, and Shaolei Ren. “Small Bottle, Big Pipe: Quantifying and Addressing the Impact of Data Centers on Public Water Systems.” arXiv:2603.02705, 2026.

So far, we have discussed why data centers use water and how much water they use over a year.

Those annual totals give us an important starting point. To understand the effect on a community, we also need to ask when that water is needed and whether the local water system can deliver it.

A utility has to supply enough water on the hottest day, while continuing to serve homes and other businesses. A relatively low annual total can still come with a large summer peak.

This is the perspective I want to introduce through this paper. I will focus on water used directly at data centers, and connect their annual water use to the capacity of local public water systems.

Let me first return briefly to the annual numbers, with an important distinction in how we read them.

The paper brings together data for five hyperscale operators and seven colocation providers. Hyperscale operators run large facilities for their own services. Colocation providers supply space, power, and cooling for their customers’ equipment.

In the selected hyperscale operations, annual water withdrawals were about forty-one billion liters. About thirty-one billion liters were consumed. For the selected colocation operations, withdrawals were about twelve billion liters, with almost ten billion liters consumed.

These are substantial volumes. But these rows describe the operators covered by the study. The sample covers about eighty-six percent of hyperscale IT load, compared with twenty-two percent of colocation IT load. So the difference between the rows does not tell us which entire sector uses more water nationally.

The distinction between withdrawal and consumption is also important. Withdrawal is the water supplied to the facility. Consumption is the portion that does not promptly return as liquid water, mainly because it evaporates.

Look at the last column. In both groups, close to four-fifths of the withdrawn water is consumed. That high fraction tells us something about the effect of this water use, beyond the annual volume alone.

Much of the withdrawn water evaporates

  • Consumptive ratio = consumption ÷ withdrawal
  • Many sites fall around 70–90%
  • A high ratio does not mean poor management: more reuse cycles can lower withdrawal and raise the ratio

Figure 1c gives us a broader view of that fraction across companies and individual sites.

The paper calls it the consumptive ratio. In plain language, it measures how much of the supplied water does not return as liquid water after use.

Many of the values are around seventy to ninety percent, and some are higher. The figure includes a range of sites and company averages, so we should expect variation. But the high ratios appear across multiple observations.

The reason follows from the cooling process we discussed earlier. Water carries heat away by evaporating. The evaporation does useful work, while also reducing the amount of liquid water available for discharge or subsequent use.

An important detail is that a high consumptive ratio does not automatically mean poor water management. A cooling tower may reuse its circulating water more times and discharge less wastewater. That can increase the fraction consumed, even while reducing total withdrawals.

So we need to keep the ratio and the total volume in view together. To understand how unusual these ratios are, it helps to compare them with other public water users.

A high consumptive ratio among public water users

  • Public supply averages about 12%
  • Evaporative data centers: about 70–90%
  • Per 100 gallons withdrawn: ~12 vs. ~75 gallons consumed

Section 3.1.1 provides some useful reference points.

Urban homes typically consume about five to fifteen percent of the water they withdraw. Office buildings are around ten percent, and manufacturing is around twenty percent in the comparisons cited by the paper. The overall public water supply average is about twelve percent.

Data centers using evaporative cooling often have much higher ratios, around seventy to ninety percent.

Imagine supplying one hundred gallons to each user. At a twelve percent consumptive ratio, about twelve gallons would be consumed. At a seventy-five percent ratio, about seventy-five gallons would be consumed. The remaining water can return as wastewater, subject to treatment and local conditions.

This comparison helps explain why equal withdrawal volumes can have different effects on downstream water availability. During a drought, that difference can matter a great deal.

It also gives us a reason to look carefully at water use, rather than treating every gallon supplied as equivalent in its effects. At the same time, we need to understand the benefit that evaporation provides. The next two panels connect this water use to weather and electricity demand.

Cooling water use rises during hot periods

  • WUE: liters consumed per kWh of IT energy
  • IT load stayed nearly constant all year
  • Water intensity peaks in early summer

Figure 1a shows monthly water use at a data center in Phoenix.

The vertical axis is water usage effectiveness, or WUE. Here, it means liters of water consumed for each kilowatt-hour of IT energy. It lets us compare water use relative to the amount of computing energy.

The facility’s IT load was nearly constant, but its water intensity changed substantially across the year. Water intensity rises into the hotter months and reaches its highest level in early summer.

That is useful evidence because it connects the seasonal pattern to cooling conditions, even when computing demand remains relatively stable.

Humidity also affects cooling performance, so the exact pattern depends on local weather and the cooling system. The clear result shown here is that cooling water demand can rise substantially during hot periods.

There is also an important difference between cooling designs. A cooling tower may use water throughout the year. A system that adds evaporative assistance only during hot weather can concentrate its water demand into a much shorter period.

Before turning to those sharper peaks, let us look at why an operator would choose to use water in the first place.

Evaporative cooling can reduce electricity demand

  • PUE: total facility energy ÷ IT energy
  • Summer PUE: about 2.0 dry vs. 1.6 evaporative
  • Water use can improve energy efficiency
  • Two different facilities, so cooling is not the only cause

Figure 1b helps explain the value of that water use.

It compares two data centers in Phoenix. One uses dry cooling, and the other uses evaporative cooling. The vertical axis is power usage effectiveness, or PUE. PUE compares the energy used by the whole facility with the energy used by the IT equipment. A lower value means less energy overhead.

The dry-cooled facility has a higher PUE, especially in summer. Its summer value approaches two, while the facility using evaporative cooling stays around one point six.

These are two different facilities, so the comparison does not isolate cooling technology as the only cause. It illustrates a tradeoff that the paper also documents through other sources: evaporation can lower the electricity needed to remove heat, particularly during hot conditions.

This changes how we should interpret cooling water. It can be a productive resource that improves energy efficiency and helps reduce demand on the grid when electricity demand is already high.

The benefit depends on the site and the cooling design. The decision also has to account for local water availability. A system that saves electricity still needs a reliable water supply.

That brings us to the central infrastructure question: how much water must the utility be ready to supply during the peak?

Data center water demand has a high peak

  • Peaking factor = peak demand ÷ average demand
  • Monthly peak-to-average ratio: 4.30 at the Iowa site
  • Estimated daily factor: at least 6.45
  • Most other large users sit near 1–2

To describe the size of a peak, the paper uses a simple measure: peak demand divided by average demand.

A ratio of two means that peak demand is twice the average. The time period matters, so we need to distinguish monthly peaks from daily peaks.

Figure 2c compares monthly peak-to-average ratios among large users served by the same waterworks. The data center is the orange bar on the right, with a ratio of four point three. Many other users are much closer to one or two, although some also have larger peaks.

That means the data center’s highest monthly withdrawal was more than four times its average monthly withdrawal over the period studied.

For infrastructure planning, utilities also need to consider the highest day. The paper applies a planning adjustment to estimate a daily ratio of at least six point four five for this case. That daily value is an estimate based on monthly data, rather than a directly measured daily peak.

The broader lesson is that a large gap can exist between average water use and the demand a utility must prepare to meet. Using water only during hot periods can reduce the annual total while leaving a substantial peak.

We can see how that happens by looking at the monthly record behind this comparison.

Water demand concentrates in summer

  • Highest month in 2024: about 20 million gallons
  • Average month: about 5 million gallons
  • The summer surge recurs every year
  • Monthly totals smooth out the highest days

Figure 2a shows monthly withdrawals for a hyperscale data center in Iowa over several years.

Water use is relatively low during the cooler months. It then rises sharply during summer, before falling again toward winter.

Look at the green line for 2024. In its highest month, the facility withdrew about twenty million gallons. The average monthly withdrawal across the series is around five million gallons. Those approximate values make the large peak-to-average ratio easy to see.

The pattern also repeats across years, although the timing and size of the peak vary. This is a recurring seasonal demand that the utility has to plan around.

And these are monthly totals. They smooth out the variation between individual days. The highest daily withdrawal can place a stronger demand on the system than the monthly average suggests.

For the operator, using water mainly during hot periods can help keep annual use low. For the water utility, those summer peaks remain an essential part of the service requirement. The next panel shows how large this demand becomes relative to other local users.

One facility can become a major local water user

  • Up to about 30% of the top-20 users’ withdrawals in summer
  • The utility’s largest annual user in 2024–25
  • Denominator: the 20 largest users, not the whole city

Figure 2b puts that seasonal demand in a local context.

It shows the data center’s share of the combined withdrawals of the twenty largest users served by the same waterworks. That denominator is important. This is a comparison with the largest users, rather than the water use of the whole city.

During cooler months, the data center accounts for only a small share. In some summer months, its share rises to around thirty percent. One facility then represents almost a third of the withdrawals within this group of large customers.

The summer demand is large enough that the paper identifies the facility as the waterworks’ largest annual water user in both 2024 and 2025.

This helps explain why a modest national percentage can coexist with a substantial local effect. Water demand is concentrated in particular communities, and the largest withdrawals can occur during a limited part of the year.

The utility has to serve that demand through its own local infrastructure. So the next question is how much additional capacity that system actually has available.

Local water capacity can limit data center growth

  • Nearly all U.S. hyperscale and colocation sites rely on community water systems
  • Source, treatment, pumps and pipes can each limit supply
  • Indiana: one campus secures 8 of 25 MGD; full delivery expected 2031
  • Storage smooths short peaks, not a prolonged heatwave

The paper finds that nearly all U.S. hyperscale and colocation data centers rely on community water systems. Much of this supply is treated drinking water, although some facilities also use reclaimed water.

That dependence makes the local system central to the discussion. Water has to come from a suitable source, pass through treatment, and reach the facility through pumps and pipes. A constraint at any stage can limit what the utility can supply.

Existing users and approved future connections also have claims on that capacity. For a new project, the relevant number is the capacity still available after those commitments and safety requirements.

The Indiana example in the paper shows the scale involved. One data center campus has an allocation of eight million gallons per day. That is thirty-two percent of the twenty-five million gallons per day of new supply capacity planned for the district.

In the plans discussed by the paper, delivery of the full water allocation is expected in 2031. Infrastructure timing can therefore affect how quickly the campus reaches full operation.

This is where high seasonal demand and local supply dependence come together. The facility needs a large water supply during hot conditions, and the host community must have the capacity to deliver it while serving other users.

Storage tanks can help smooth short peaks. A prolonged heatwave is harder to cover with storage alone. That is why the paper treats water capacity as a potentially significant constraint on future data center growth.

The paper then asks what this pattern could mean as U.S. data centers expand through 2030.

The projections combine different rates of computing growth with different assumptions about water intensity. In the baseline, water consumed per unit of IT energy stays at its 2024 level. The moderate and optimistic scenarios assume annual reductions of five and ten percent, respectively.

These are scenarios that show how the result changes with growth and efficiency. They do not represent a single prediction of what will happen.

Under the baseline, annual withdrawals in 2030 reach roughly eighty to one hundred and fifty billion gallons across the low- and high-growth cases. Both hyperscale and colocation facilities contribute to this demand.

The paper compares that with the public water supply benchmark it uses. Data centers would account for roughly zero point six to one point one percent of withdrawals, but around four to seven percent of consumptive use. The higher consumption share follows from the high consumptive ratios we saw earlier.

For the national capacity analysis, the paper uses a planning peak-to-average factor of four point five. This reflects the need to prepare for high-demand conditions, rather than size the system around an ordinary day.

Under the baseline, data center growth between 2024 and 2030 requires roughly seven hundred to fourteen hundred and fifty million gallons per day of additional water capacity.

For scale, New York City’s average daily water supply is about one thousand million gallons. The estimated additional capacity is therefore comparable in size to the daily supply of a very large city.

This is a comparison of scale. The estimate adds together capacity requirements in different places. Their peaks do not necessarily occur on the same day, and the facilities would not use that much water every day.

The optimistic scenario assumes a ten percent reduction in water intensity every year. Additional capacity still ranges from about two hundred and thirty to six hundred million gallons per day. Efficiency makes a substantial difference, while rapid computing growth continues to create a need for capacity.

The paper also estimates the value of the additional capacity. In the high-growth baseline, that valuation ranges from fifteen to fifty-eight billion dollars. This is an estimate of infrastructure value, rather than a precise bill for new funding, since some capacity may already exist or have financing.

For an individual water utility, the practical challenge remains local: securing capacity, financing improvements, and completing the work in time. Those challenges lead directly to the paper’s recommendations.

Planning for peak water demand

01Report site-level peak water demandAlongside annual totals, so utilities know what they must be ready to serve
02Co-invest in local water infrastructureFund upgrades, share technical support, use reclaimed water where suitable
03Water Capacity Neutral (“Pipe Neutral”)Add usable local capacity that covers the site’s allocation
04Plan water and power togetherUse water when the grid is stressed; save water when capacity is tight

The paper closes with four recommendations that follow from this capacity perspective.

First, operators should report peak water demand for individual sites, alongside annual totals. Utilities need to know the demand they must be ready to serve. Annual fleet averages provide only part of that information.

Second, operators and communities should work together on infrastructure. That includes funding upgrades and providing technical support. Reclaimed water can reduce demand for drinking water, although it still needs suitable treatment and delivery capacity.

Third, the paper proposes Water Capacity Neutral commitments, also called Pipe Neutral. The idea is for a data center to add enough usable local capacity to cover its allocation, while allowing the community to retain capacity for other needs and future growth. This complements efforts to replenish water volumes.

Finally, water and power planning should happen together. Evaporative cooling can help reduce electricity demand when the grid is under pressure. When water capacity is tight and electricity is available, operators may have opportunities to use less water. The appropriate choice depends on the conditions of both systems.

For this part of the talk, the main point is that annual water totals give us only part of the information we need. Understanding the effect on a community also requires peak demand and the local system’s ability to meet it. That perspective helps us preserve the energy benefits of cooling water while planning for its infrastructure needs.

We just saw the Omaha story. So where does a data center's air pollution actually come from? There are three sources.

First, offsite: the power plants that feed the grid. This is scope 2. Fossil-fuel power plants are the largest stationary source of NOx in the U.S. In Omaha, coal units stayed online past their retirement date because of new data center load.

Second, onsite backup diesel generators. They are meant for outages, but they are tested every month, and regulators are now asking data centers to run them during grid alerts. Northern Virginia alone has about 4,600 of them, around 11 gigawatts, which is more than many large power plants combined.

Third, and growing fastest, onsite gas turbines. Data centers that cannot wait years for a grid connection bring their own power. Global Energy Monitor counted about 100 gigawatts of behind-the-meter gas for data centers in development at the start of 2026, up from 4 gigawatts in early 2024. The best-known example is xAI's Colossus in Memphis: aerial images in April 2025 showed 35 turbines, while the permit later covered 15. At the public hearing, a Boxtown resident with asthma held up her inhaler and asked: how come I can't breathe at home and y'all get to breathe at home?

So, to recap, we've focused on three key environmental footprints of AI computing: carbon emissions, which contribute to climate change; water consumption, which intensifies water stress; and air pollution, which adversely affects public health. A critical finding is that all three of these footprints exhibit significant temporal and geographical variation. This variability isn't just a challenge; it presents a substantial opportunity to strategically schedule AI computing by shifting workloads across different times and locations to minimize their environmental impact.

Next, let's look at a demo from Google that illustrates the concept of geographical workload management. As you'll see, Google operates multiple data centers hosting AI services. Because the availability of renewable energy varies by location, some data centers inherently have a lower environmental footprint than others. By strategically shifting workloads between these locations, it's possible to reduce the overall environmental burden while maintaining a seamless user experience. This approach is also commonly referred to as geographical load balancing

The first recommendation is straightforward: annual reports should include site-level peak metrics. Utilities need maximum-day withdrawal and discharge, because those quantities help determine supply and wastewater infrastructure. The authors also propose peak water usage effectiveness, or pWUE: the maximum, across days, of that day’s water consumption divided by that day’s IT energy. It is a consumption metric, so it cannot replace peak withdrawal capacity in MGD. The illustration follows Appendix C: at roughly flat IT load, annual WUE of 0.15 combined with a peaking factor of 6.5 gives pWUE near one liter per kilowatt-hour. These are illustrative assumptions. Reporting should distinguish an observed peak, an estimated design peak, and a contractual allocation. A utility also needs hourly or instantaneous design flows where those constrain the network. Publishing these quantities together would make the actual planning problem much more visible.

Cooling choices link water and power peaks

  • Dry cooling can increase electricity demand in hot periods
  • Evaporative assistance can reduce power peaks where water allows
  • Surplus power may reduce water demand when grid headroom exists

PACING: 105 seconds.

The paper argues for coordinating water and power planning because a water constraint can shift pressure onto the grid. This historical comparison shows two Phoenix facilities, one using dry cooling and one using evaporative cooling. The dry-cooled facility’s PUE rises more strongly in summer. These are different facilities, so the plot is not a controlled estimate of a universal technology effect. It illustrates the tradeoff the paper develops using additional disclosures. Where water capacity is available, evaporative assistance can help reduce peak electricity demand. Where the water system is stressed and electrical headroom exists, more dry cooling may help reduce water demand. The proposed use of surplus power is conditional: solar production at midday does not guarantee spare grid capacity during a heatwave. A joint operating strategy needs both systems’ constraints. Lower direct water use can be valuable while still requiring an explicit assessment of the resulting electricity demand.

Fairness-aware geographical load balancing

Each hour t:

  1. observe demand yt and local conditions at
  2. route requests xt, without knowing the future
Hitting cost f(xt, yt)Energy cost of serving this hour’s requests at local electricity prices
Switching cost c(xt, xt−1)Penalty for shifting load abruptly between hours
Footprint p(xt, at)Water and carbon of each data center, from its WUE and grid carbon intensity at
Long-term cost h(·)Footprint of the most burdened region over the whole period (minimax fairness)

Routing is decided hour by hour, but fairness is judged on each region’s total over T.

Li, Pengfei, Jianyi Yang, Adam Wierman, and Shaolei Ren. "Towards Environmentally Equitable AI via Geographical Load Balancing." In Proceedings of
the 15th ACM International Conference on Future and Sustainable Energy Systems, pp. 291-307. 2024.

We route AI inference requests across data centers every hour. The first two terms are the usual GLB costs: energy cost, and a penalty for moving load too abruptly. The new term is h: it looks at each region’s water and carbon footprint accumulated over the whole period and penalizes the most burdened region. The difficulty is that we must decide each hour without knowing future demand, prices, or water efficiency, while h depends on the whole horizon.
